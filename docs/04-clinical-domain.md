Phase 2.5 — Clinical Domain Model & Workflow Design
Before the document itself, one reconciliation I need to make explicit, because your brief asks for a "Health Graph" that "replaces the traditional timeline concept" — but Phase 1.1 already established Health Journey as a named, differentiator-level concept. These aren't actually in conflict, but left unresolved they would quietly become two competing mental models for the same data. I'm resolving this now: the Health Graph is the underlying clinical data model; a Health Journey is a doctor-curated narrative view over a connected subgraph. The Graph is the substrate; Journeys are how humans (patients and doctors) read it. This is the single most important architectural decision in this document, so I'm stating it up front rather than burying it in Section 3.

1. Clinical Philosophy

Doctors treat patients, not screens. Every workspace decision is judged by whether it gets software out of the way of the clinical encounter, not by how much information it can display.
AI assists but never practices medicine. Restated once more because it is the load-bearing constraint of this entire platform — not a caveat, the foundation.
Every action preserves clinical context. A doctor should never have to reconstruct "why am I looking at this" — the system carries context forward (from booking reason → consultation → note → prescription) rather than treating each as an isolated transaction.
Medical records evolve, never get rewritten. Corrections are additions with provenance, not overwrites — this was already a Phase 1.2 trust rule; it's restated here because it's also a clinical-safety rule, not just a trust one (a silently altered record is a patient-safety hazard, not only a trust violation).
Every important clinical decision is traceable. Who decided what, when, based on what information, with what AI input if any — this must be reconstructable after the fact, always.
The record serves the next doctor, not just the current one. Documentation quality is judged by whether an unfamiliar doctor picking up this patient's care in a year would understand the story, not just whether today's note is technically complete.
New principle — Uncertainty must be representable. Real medicine has ambiguous diagnoses, differential considerations, and "watchful waiting" states. A domain model that only supports confirmed, clean diagnoses will force doctors to document false certainty, which is itself a patient-safety risk. The model must have a first-class way to represent "suspected, unconfirmed, ruled out" — not just "diagnosed."
New principle — The platform augments care, it does not own it. For emergencies, second opinions, and anything genuinely outside its designed scope, the system's job is to route to appropriate real-world care, not to simulate handling everything itself. This becomes critical in Section 10.

Key clinical-workflow correction to your proposed order: Lab Requests and Radiology are drawn as sequential steps after Prescription in your brief, but clinically this is often reversed or parallel — a doctor frequently orders labs/imaging before committing to a treatment plan or prescription, to confirm a suspected diagnosis first. The lifecycle above should be read as a typical path with a strong "assessment ⇄ labs/imaging ⇄ diagnosis" loop, not a strict one-way pipeline. I'd recommend the actual data model support Lab/Radiology requests being creatable from the Assessment stage directly, with Diagnosis and Treatment Plan potentially waiting on their results (an "awaiting results" consultation state, not a hard blocker to closing the encounter if follow-up is scheduled instead).

3. Health Graph
Why a graph beats a flat timeline, and why it also beats a set of siloed Journeys: A timeline answers "what happened, in what order." A graph answers "how is everything connected" — which is the question that actually matters clinically. A patient's hypertension, their prescribed beta-blocker, a resulting fatigue symptom, and an unrelated but concurrent vitamin D deficiency are not independent timeline entries; they're a small connected structure of cause, effect, and coincidence that a doctor needs to reason about together.
Node types: Conditions/Diagnoses, Symptoms, Medications, Lab Results, Visits/Consultations, Doctors, Procedures, Allergies.
Edge types (this is where the real clinical value lives):

diagnosed_during (Condition ← Visit)
treated_with (Condition → Medication)
caused_by / contraindicated_by (Symptom ← Medication, for side effects)
confirmed_by / suggests (Condition ← Lab Result, with a certainty weight per Principle 7)
treated_by (Condition → Doctor, historical)
related_to (Condition ↔ Condition, e.g., diabetes and hypertension as comorbid, non-causally-proven-but-clinically-relevant links)
part_of (sub-condition → parent condition, resolving the parent/child question flagged as open in Phase 1.2 — this is where that question gets its answer: a Journey is a curated traversal path through part_of and diagnosed_during/treated_with edges rooted at one primary condition node, and complications become child nodes linked via part_of, giving us both a clean Journey narrative and an honest graph structure underneath, resolving the tension without forcing an artificial either/or).

How multiple conditions interact: The graph lets a doctor see, for instance, that a patient's new joint pain symptom sits adjacent to both their existing autoimmune condition node and a recently started medication node — a flat timeline would show these as three separate entries in a list; the graph surfaces the relationship directly, which is exactly the kind of pattern a doctor needs seconds into a consultation, not something they have to mentally reconstruct.
How chronic diseases evolve: A chronic condition node persists with a changing state over time (active, controlled, worsening, resolved) with each visit/lab-result node attaching as evidence for that state change — this is structurally the same information as a Health Journey's stage progression (Phase 1.1), just now formally represented as graph traversal rather than a separate concept.
How AI should understand the graph: The AI Copilot's context window for any suggestion should be the locally relevant subgraph (the current condition being discussed, its directly connected medications/symptoms/labs, and any comorbid conditions with known interaction risk) — not the patient's entire history indiscriminately, which would be both computationally wasteful and clinically noisy. This is an important architectural constraint for Phase 3+: AI Copilot suggestions must be scoped to a graph traversal radius, not "everything about this patient."
How doctors navigate it: Doctors should not be expected to interact with a literal node-and-edge graph visualization during a live consultation — that would violate Calm Interfaces and add cognitive load exactly when doctors have the least attention to spare. The graph is the backend model; the Journey view (a clean, linear, Apple-Health-style stage progression) is the primary UI surface, with the full graph available as an optional "explore connections" view for complex cases — this is the resolution to the tension named at the top of this document, made concrete.

4. Clinical Workspace ("IDE for Clinicians")
The IDE metaphor is a genuinely good one, and worth taking seriously rather than just adopting as a marketing phrase — a good IDE's core property isn't "shows everything," it's context-aware panels that surface exactly what's relevant to what you're doing right now, with everything else one keystroke away. That's the actual design target, not a wall of simultaneously-maximized panels.
Persistent elements (always visible, minimal): Video pane, patient identity header (name, age, active Journey being discussed), a slim AI Copilot presence indicator (collapsed by default, per Phase 1.1's "quiet state" requirement).
Contextual panels (appear based on workflow stage, collapsible): Patient 360 summary (Section 5), active Health Journey view, Notes/documentation panel, Prescription builder, Lab/Radiology request builder, AI Copilot expanded panel.
Working context concept: The Workspace should maintain a single "current focus" state — e.g., "we are discussing the Hypertension Journey" — and every panel (notes, AI suggestions, prescription drafting) inherits that focus automatically, the way an IDE's file tree, terminal, and editor all share awareness of "which project/file you're in." This is the concrete mechanism behind "zero context switching" — it's not that everything is visible at once, it's that everything present is scoped to the same working context without the doctor needing to re-select it repeatedly.
Communication: In-consultation chat and any async messaging thread tied to this patient are reachable from the same workspace, not a separate inbox requiring navigation away.
Documentation: Note-taking happens inline, structured (SOAP-aware), with AI-drafted content appearing as clearly-marked suggested text the doctor edits in place — never a separate "review AI output" modal that breaks flow.
How doctors stay "in flow": By minimizing state loss — every panel remembers where the doctor left off if collapsed and reopened, the working context persists across the whole encounter, and nothing requires a full page navigation/reload. This is a real engineering constraint for Phase 3+, not just a design intention.

5. Patient 360
Priority and hierarchy — what a doctor needs in the first 5 seconds vs. the first 30 seconds vs. on demand:
Tier 1 (instant, always visible): Clinical Alerts (allergies, critical interactions, anything AI-flagged as high-risk), Current Medications, active Health Journey(s) relevant to today's visit reason.
Tier 2 (one glance away, visible on panel expand): AI Summary (a concise, doctor-oriented synthesis of relevant history — not a patient-facing summary, a different register entirely), Recent Visits, Vitals trend, relevant Lab History.
Tier 3 (available on demand, not pre-loaded into the doctor's attention): Full Health Passport (Lifestyle, Nutrition, Exercise, Family History, full Mental Health section subject to consent, Insurance, Radiology archive, Previous Doctors list).
Why this hierarchy, explicitly: A doctor treating a skin condition doesn't need the patient's full nutrition log in their immediate field of view, but does need to know about a drug allergy instantly regardless of specialty. Getting this priority ordering right is arguably more important to actual clinical safety than any single feature in this document — a Patient 360 that buries an allergy under equally-weighted lifestyle data has failed at its one job.
Mental Health handling within Patient 360, restated from Phase 1.1's consent rule: even when consented and visible, Mental Health data should sit in Tier 3, not Tier 1/2, unless the current consultation's stated reason is mental-health-related — this respects both the sensitivity of the data and the "don't overload Tier 1" principle simultaneously.

6. Doctor 360
Why each area exists, briefly, since most were established in earlier phases and just need consolidating here:

Portfolio — the doctor's public professional identity (Phase 1.1); exists to build durable reputation.
Availability — direct control over their own time/liquidity contribution to the platform.
Patients — their own active patient panel, distinct from the platform's aggregate patient base; exists so a doctor can manage an ongoing practice, not just isolated bookings.
Analytics — practice performance visibility (Phase 1.2's Doctor Intelligence domain).
Knowledge Center — reputation-building and patient engagement beyond consultations.
Consultation History — their own clinical record-keeping across all past encounters, for continuity and self-review.
AI Insights — Doctor Intelligence suggestions (response time, content topic ideas, scheduling optimization) — deliberately a different AI surface from the Clinical Intelligence Copilot used during consultations, per the domain separation established in Phase 1.2.
Reputation — reviews and endorsements, aggregated.
Revenue — earnings, payouts, commission transparency (Stripe-inspired clarity, per Phase 1.1's design philosophy).
Growth — practice-growth-oriented metrics (new patients, follow-up conversion) — distinct from Analytics in framing (Analytics = "how am I doing," Growth = "how do I do more").
Professional Development — a new area worth naming explicitly here: continuing education tracking, certifications earned, conference participation — this ties directly to the Portfolio's credibility model and gives doctors a reason to keep their profile a living document rather than a static one-time submission.

8. AI Copilot Workflow
The governing question for every AI behavior below: does this observation/suggestion reduce doctor cognitive load, or add to it? If uncertain, the AI stays silent. This is the operating rule beneath everything that follows.
When AI observes (passive, no visible output): Continuously during a live consultation — transcribing speech, tracking mentioned symptoms/medications against the Health Graph — but produces nothing visible unless a threshold below is crossed. Observation without surfacing is the default state, not an exception.
When AI suggests (active, low-urgency, dismissible without friction): Suggested follow-up questions during assessment, SOAP note drafts after the encounter, prescription drafts, suggested lab tests. These appear in the contextual side panel (Phase 1.1), are visually distinct as "draft/suggestion," and require zero action if the doctor ignores them — silence from the doctor is a valid response, not a stalled state.
When AI warns (active, higher-urgency, requires acknowledgment): Drug interaction alerts, allergy conflicts, anything that could cause direct patient harm if missed. These interrupt more assertively than a passive suggestion (per Phase 2's edge case 61) and require an explicit acknowledge-or-override action with a logged justification if overridden — this is the one category where "just ignore it" is not an acceptable default interaction, because the cost of a missed warning is categorically higher than the cost of a missed suggestion.
When AI summarizes: At natural transition points — pre-consultation (Doctor Preparation stage), post-consultation (Journey Update stage), and on-demand when a doctor opens the AI Summary tier of Patient 360. Summarization is a distinct mode from live suggestion — it's retrospective synthesis, not real-time nudging, and should be visually/interactionally distinguishable so a doctor never confuses "here's what happened" with "here's what to do next."
When AI remains silent — this deserves equal design weight to when it speaks: During emotionally sensitive disclosure (Section 7's Psychiatry note, extendable to any specialty when a doctor manually invokes a "quiet mode"), when confidence in a suggestion is below a defined threshold (better to say nothing than to offer a low-confidence guess dressed as a suggestion), and by default for anything outside the Clinical Intelligence domain's approved suggestion types (Phase 1.2) — the AI does not freelance into commentary outside its defined scope, ever.
How AI understands consultation context: Via the "current working context" concept from Section 4 — the AI's suggestion scope is bound to whatever Journey/condition is currently in focus, not the patient's entire history indiscriminately (restated from Section 3, because it's the same architectural constraint applied here specifically to interaction design rather than data scope).
How AI understands Health Graph relationships: By traversing the locally relevant subgraph (Section 3) to ground its suggestions — e.g., a drug interaction warning is generated by checking the proposed medication node against the patient's existing medication nodes and known allergy nodes directly, not through unconstrained generative inference. This distinction matters enormously for safety: the interaction/allergy-checking capability should be architected as deterministic rule/data lookup against structured graph data, with generative AI used only for the drafting and language around that lookup (e.g., explaining the interaction in plain language) — never for deciding whether an interaction exists. Conflating these two things (using a language model to "guess" at interactions rather than checking a maintained clinical database) is the single most dangerous architectural mistake this platform could make, and it's worth stating this distinction explicitly and permanently in this foundational document.
How AI avoids interrupting clinicians: Suggestions accumulate quietly in the collapsed side panel (Phase 1.1's "quiet state") rather than popping up as modal interruptions; only Warnings (the one urgent category above) are permitted to visually assert themselves more strongly, and even then, non-blockingly — a warning should never freeze the interface or prevent the doctor from continuing to work while acknowledging it.

9. Clinical State Machines
Presented concisely — states, valid transitions, and the notable invalid/exceptional cases, since the full transition-table format would be more useful as a diagram in Phase 3 than as prose here.
Appointment: Requested → Confirmed → (Rescheduled | Cancelled | No-Show | Completed). Invalid: Confirmed → Requested (no backward transition — a cancelled/rescheduled appointment creates a new Confirmed state, it doesn't revert). Exceptional: Confirmed → Disputed (payment/technical failure requiring admin resolution, Phase 2 edge cases).
Consultation: Scheduled → Waiting Room → In Progress → (Completed | Interrupted) → Closed. Exceptional: In Progress → Emergency Escalation (Section 10) which can resolve back to In Progress or terminate the consultation state entirely depending on real-world outcome. Invalid: Closed → In Progress (a closed consultation cannot reopen — corrections happen via addendum per Principle 4, not by reopening the encounter).
Prescription: Draft (AI-suggested) → Doctor-Edited → Signed/Issued → (Active | Expired | Superseded). Invalid: any direct Draft → Signed transition without a doctor-review step being logged (the system must record that the doctor actively engaged with the draft, addressing the "approval fatigue" risk flagged in Phase 2). Exceptional: Signed → Disputed if a patient/pharmacy flags an issue, requiring doctor review, never silent edit of an issued prescription (a correction issues a new superseding prescription instead).
Health Graph node (e.g., a Condition): Suspected → Confirmed → (Active | Controlled | Worsening | Resolved), with Ruled Out reachable from Suspected directly. Invalid: Resolved → Suspected (a resolved condition reactivating creates a new linked node referencing the prior one, preserving history rather than erasing the resolution). This directly operationalizes Principle 7 (uncertainty must be representable).
Lab/Radiology Request: Requested → (Awaiting Fulfillment | Fulfilled Externally) → Results Uploaded → Reviewed by Doctor → Linked to Graph. Exceptional: Requested → Abandoned if a patient never fulfills it — this state matters, since an unfulfilled request left invisible is itself a patient-safety gap the doctor should be able to see (e.g., a "3 open lab requests, none fulfilled" indicator).
Follow-up: Scheduled → (Attended | Missed)  or Conditional (symptom-triggered) → Activated → Booked. Invalid: a follow-up cannot silently expire without notification — a missed follow-up should always surface back to both patient and doctor.
Doctor Verification: Submitted → Under Review → (Approved | Rejected | More Info Needed) , with Approved → Re-Verification Due on a periodic or triggered basis (license renewal, specialty change, per Phase 2 edge cases). Exceptional: Approved → Suspended (admin action) → Reinstated | Permanently Revoked.
Patient Consent: Not Granted → Granted (scoped: per-doctor, per-category) → Revoked, with all states versioned (Phase 2's edge case 100 requirement) so historical consent state at any past point in time is reconstructable. Invalid: revocation retroactively altering already-completed clinical actions (per Section 3's earlier rule — revocation is forward-only).
Knowledge Article: Draft → (Pre-Publication Review | Published directly, per doctor trust tier) → Published → (Flagged → Under Review → Reinstated | Removed) . Exceptional: Published → Needs Update (content-freshness prompt, Phase 2 edge case 75) — this is a non-blocking state, the article stays live while flagged for author review.

10. Emergency Workflow
This deserved dedicated design attention flagged as a gap in Phase 2 — here it is.
Detection: Emergency signals can arise two ways, and the design must handle both: (1) doctor-identified — the doctor recognizes an emergency during the conversation (chest pain, stroke symptoms, severe allergic reaction, loss of consciousness risk, suicidal ideation) and manually triggers Emergency Mode; (2) AI-flagged — the AI Copilot's passive observation (Section 8) detects emergency-pattern language in the transcript (e.g., explicit self-harm statements) and surfaces a prominent, immediate alert to the doctor — but never acts autonomously, only surfaces to the doctor for their judgment call, consistent with every AI principle established so far.
Doctor experience: A single, always-reachable "Emergency Protocol" control within the Workspace (not buried in a menu — this needs to be as accessible as the video controls themselves). Activating it surfaces: the patient's Emergency Contact info (Health Passport, overriding normal consent restrictions per the established exception), local emergency service numbers relevant to the patient's stated location, and a structured brief on recommended next steps (directing to in-person/emergency care — the platform's job here per Principle 8 is to route, not to manage the emergency itself).
Patient experience: Calm, directive, non-alarming language from the doctor (a UX/training concern more than a software one) — the software's role is to make sure the doctor has what they need instantly, not to interject its own messaging into the patient-facing video call, which would be jarring and could undermine the doctor's own handling of the moment.
AI behavior during Emergency Mode: Suggestion-panel AI Copilot activity should go quiet/deprioritized here — this is the highest-stakes moment for the "when AI remains silent" principle in Section 8; the doctor's full attention should go to the patient and the emergency protocol, not to reviewing draft suggestions.
Platform behavior: The consultation transitions to an Emergency Escalation state (Section 9); billing/normal-completion logic is suspended for this session (Emergency Mode should never be a moment where the platform is thinking about payment); the encounter, once resolved, requires mandatory structured documentation (what was observed, what action was recommended, what the outcome was, to the extent known) — this record has real legal and clinical-continuity importance and should not be optional or skippable.
Escalation and audit requirements: Every Emergency Mode activation is logged with full detail (audit trail, per Trust Layer) and should trigger a mandatory admin-visible flag for review — not because the doctor did anything wrong, but because emergency-adjacent encounters are exactly the category of event the platform's leadership and legal/compliance function need visibility into as a matter of institutional responsibility, regardless of outcome.
Honest limitation to state plainly, not paper over: A video consultation platform has real, hard boundaries around what it can do in a true emergency — it cannot dispatch an ambulance, it cannot guarantee the patient acts on the recommendation, and it cannot verify the patient's actual physical location reliably in all cases. The design goal is to make the doctor as effective as possible in that moment, not to pretend the software itself is an emergency response system. This honesty should be reflected in doctor onboarding/training materials, not just the software design.

11. Product Language (Concept Glossary)

Health Passport — the patient's lifelong, patient-owned medical identity; the container/interface for their health information.
Health Graph — the underlying connected data model (conditions, medications, symptoms, labs, visits, doctors) that gives the Health Passport its clinical depth; the substrate beneath the Passport.
Health Journey — a doctor-curated, human-readable narrative path through a specific portion of the Health Graph, rooted at one primary condition (with linked sub-conditions via part_of edges); how patients and doctors actually read the graph without needing to think in graph terms.
Doctor Portfolio — the doctor's public professional identity and accumulated reputation.
Doctor Workspace / Clinical Workspace — the doctor's unified, context-aware working environment during and around consultations (the "IDE for clinicians").
Patient 360 — the tiered, prioritized view of everything relevant about a patient, surfaced within the Workspace.
Doctor 360 — the doctor's own complete professional operating view (practice, reputation, growth, development) — distinct from the Workspace, which is patient/consultation-facing.
Knowledge Center — the platform's publishing and content-discovery layer, connecting doctor expertise to patient engagement between consultations.
AI Copilot — the contextual, non-chat, approval-gated intelligence layer assisting (never deciding) throughout the clinical workflow.
Trust Layer — the cross-cutting system of verification, consent, audit, and transparency that makes every other concept above credible and safe.

How they relate, in one line each: The Health Passport is what the patient owns; the Health Graph is how it's structured; the Health Journey is how it's understood; the Doctor Workspace is where care happens; the Patient 360 is what the doctor sees there; the Doctor Portfolio and Doctor 360 are the doctor's own professional world; the Knowledge Center connects that world to patients between encounters; the AI Copilot assists throughout all of it; and the Trust Layer is the reason anyone can believe any of it is safe.

12. Final Clinical Review
Clinical risks: The deterministic-lookup-vs-generative-inference distinction for drug interactions (Section 8) is the single most safety-critical architectural decision in this document — if this gets blurred during actual implementation (e.g., an engineer under deadline pressure lets a language model "just answer" an interaction question instead of querying a maintained clinical drug database), it silently reintroduces exactly the risk this whole document exists to prevent. This needs to survive as a hard requirement all the way through Phase 3/4, not just live here as a paragraph.
Workflow risks: The Lab/Radiology sequencing correction in Section 2 (results-before-diagnosis loop, not strictly after prescription) is a real workflow risk if ignored — building the system around a rigid one-way pipeline would force doctors into artificial documentation gymnastics that don't match how they actually think, which risks poor adoption or, worse, doctors working around the system in ways that degrade record quality.
UX risks: Specialty-aware Tier 1 overrides (Section 7) are clinically correct but add real configuration complexity to the Patient 360 hierarchy (Section 5) — this needs careful Phase 3 design so it doesn't become an inconsistent, hard-to-predict experience for doctors who treat patients across contexts (e.g., a GP who occasionally handles a mental-health-adjacent visit but isn't a psychiatrist — does the Mental Health Tier-1 promotion apply to them too, based on visit reason, or only to verified psychiatry specialists? This needs an explicit answer before Phase 3: recommend visit-reason-driven promotion, not specialty-identity-driven, so any doctor discussing a mental-health-relevant visit gets the safer, more attentive default, regardless of their primary specialty label.)
Safety concerns: The Emergency Workflow's honest limitations (Section 10) need to be more than a paragraph in this document — they need to become an explicit, prominent part of doctor onboarding and platform Terms of Service, so expectations (both doctors' and the company's legal position) are set correctly before any real emergency occurs, not discovered during one.
Scalability concerns: The Health Graph's clinical value depends on consistent, structured diagnosis/condition entry — if doctors are allowed to enter free-text diagnoses without any structured backing (e.g., no controlled vocabulary/coding), the graph's edges become unreliable and its main value proposition (relationship visibility) degrades over time as the data grows messier rather than richer. This strongly suggests adopting a structured clinical coding standard (e.g., ICD-11, which Egyptian digital health guidance already points toward per Phase 1's research) for diagnosis entry from V1, not retrofitting it later once thousands of free-text diagnoses already exist.
Missing concepts: A clear definition of what happens when a diagnosis needs specialist referral outside the platform's current doctor pool (e.g., a GP recognizes something requiring in-person surgical evaluation) — the Health Graph/Journey should have a "referred out" terminal-ish state that honestly represents "this needs care we can't currently deliver," rather than forcing every Journey toward an on-platform resolution that may not be realistic.
Anything unrealistic: The literal reading of "doctor should never need to switch screens" combined with the full Patient 360 + Health Graph + specialty adaptations + AI Copilot as truly simultaneous full-detail panels would, if taken completely literally, produce a cluttered interface that violates Calm Interfaces — this tension has now been resolved twice (Phase 1.1 and again here via Tiers/working-context), and I want to flag it a third time because it's the idea most likely to be quietly over-literalized during actual screen design in Phase 3 if this resolution isn't carried forward deliberately.
Anything that would prevent doctor adoption: Approval fatigue (Phase 2 edge case 63) combined with mandatory acknowledgment for Warnings (Section 8) could, if poorly calibrated, make doctors feel micromanaged by the software rather than assisted by it — the threshold for what counts as a "Warning" requiring friction versus a "Suggestion" requiring none is a genuinely high-stakes calibration decision that deserves real doctor user-testing in Phase 3, not just internal design judgment.
Challenging our own assumption directly: This entire document assumes doctors will trust and correctly calibrate their reliance on a Health Graph/Journey system that's more structurally sophisticated than what most of them use today (many likely work from paper or basic EHR software with none of this). There's a real adoption-curve risk that the graph's sophistication is a reviewer/architecture strength but an actual working doctor friction point if the Journey-view abstraction (Section 3) doesn't hide the underlying complexity well enough. This is worth treating as a genuine open risk, not a solved problem, going into Phase 3's actual screen design.
