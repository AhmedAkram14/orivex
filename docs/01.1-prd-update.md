Phase 1.1 — PRD Update (Integration of Refined Product Decisions)
Good direction on all seven points — these push the product from "competent telemedicine app" toward something with actual defensible identity. I'm integrating each into the existing PRD structure below as deltas, not a rewrite, and flagging where a decision creates new complexity we need to own consciously rather than discover in month 4.

1. Doctor Workspace (replaces "Doctor Dashboard" — Section 2.14/2.17 update)
Reframing: A dashboard is a place you check. A workspace is a place you work. This is the right instinct, and it changes what we're actually building: not a dashboard with a video widget bolted on, but a single-screen clinical cockpit where video, history, notes, prescriptions, lab requests, and AI Copilot are panels of one coherent surface, not separate pages behind separate URLs.
Updated module definition:
Doctor Workspace
Purpose: The doctor's complete environment during and around a consultation — the single most important screen in the entire product, because this is where the doctor spends their actual working time and where clinical quality (and platform stickiness) is made or lost.
Goals: Zero context-switching during a live consultation; every piece of patient context available without navigation; low cognitive load under real-time pressure (a doctor mid-consultation has less attention to spare than a doctor filling out paperwork later).
Layout concept (conceptual, not UI design yet — that's Phase 3): A persistent video pane, a resizable/collapsible Health Passport + Health Journey panel, a Notes/Prescription/Lab-Request action panel, and the AI Copilot as a contextual side panel (per your point 4) — all visible in one viewport, not tabs.
Dependencies: Health Passport, Health Journey, Video Consultation, AI Copilot, Prescriptions, Lab Requests.
Business rules (new): The workspace must load full patient context (subject to the patient's per-doctor sharing permissions from Section 2.4) before the video call connects, not during — a doctor should never be searching for context while a patient is already waiting on camera.
Team challenge, stated directly: "Never switch screens" is the right design goal, but it's also the single hardest engineering and design constraint in this entire PRD. Real EHR-adjacent products routinely fail at this (look at how cluttered most hospital EHR software is — Epic, Cerner — precisely because "show everything at once" without a strong information hierarchy collapses into visual noise). We should treat "unified workspace" as a design principle (progressive disclosure, contextual panels that expand on demand rather than everything permanently rendered) rather than a literal requirement that all eight data types are simultaneously fully visible at all times. I'll flag this again explicitly in Phase 3 (UX) as the top design risk to get right.

2. Health Passport (renames and expands "Patient Health Profile" — Section 2.4 update)
Rename: Patient Health Profile → Health Passport throughout the PRD, Section 2.4 and all references.
Expanded content model:

Medical Timeline (existing)
Health Journeys (new — see Section 5 below, structurally part of the Passport, not separate)
Allergies, Chronic Diseases, Medications (existing, retained)
Vaccinations, Family History (existing, retained)
Lifestyle (new: smoking/alcohol status, sleep patterns, occupation-related risk factors)
Mental Health (new — see flag below)
Nutrition (new)
Exercise (new)
Wearables (future — explicitly Future/⚪, not V1, per your own labeling — good call keeping this out of scope now)
Insurance (new — informational only in V1, no claims processing, ties to future UHIS integration noted in Phase 1)
Emergency Contacts (new)

Business rules (new/updated):

Mental Health data deserves a stricter sharing default than the rest of the Passport. Recommend: mental health entries are hidden from any doctor by default and require a separate, explicit patient toggle per doctor — not bundled into the general "share my history" consent. This isn't just a UX nicety; mental health data carries disproportionate stigma and sensitivity, and treating it identically to, say, vaccination history under one consent switch is a design mistake many health apps make.
Emergency Contacts are visible to a treating doctor during an active consultation regardless of general sharing settings, since their entire purpose is acute-situation access — this is one deliberate exception to patient-controlled visibility, and should be disclosed clearly to patients so it doesn't feel like a broken promise.
Insurance data in V1 is display-only (patient's own record-keeping); no verification, no claims logic — this matches Phase 1's "insurance billing = Future" scope, just clarifying that storing insurance info is fine now, processing it isn't.

New opportunity this creates: the Health Passport, done well, becomes something patients want to export and keep even if they leave the platform — which sounds like a retention risk but is actually a trust-building feature (data portability is a HIPAA-adjacent best practice per the earlier research, and "you can always take your data with you" is a strong trust signal that increases willingness to invest data in the platform in the first place). Recommend a "Download my Health Passport" (PDF/structured export) feature — low cost, high trust dividend, worth pulling into V1 rather than deferring.

3. Doctor Portfolio (expands "Doctor Profiles" — Section 2.3 update)
Rename/expand: Doctor Profiles → Doctor Portfolio.
Expanded content model, added to existing (credentials, specialties, pricing, reviews):

Biography, Education, Certifications, Professional Experience (existing, formalized)
Publications, Research (new)
Educational Articles, Educational Videos (new — this directly connects to Knowledge Center, Section 6 below; don't build this as a separate content system, it should share infrastructure with Knowledge Center)
Awards, Conferences, Professional Memberships (new)
Languages, Response Statistics, Consultation Statistics, Reviews, Availability (existing, retained)

Business rules (new):

Publications/research claims should carry a lightweight verification tier (e.g., linking to a DOI/PubMed entry, or "self-reported, unverified" labeling) — otherwise this becomes a credential inflation vector, undermining the trust goal the whole Portfolio concept is meant to serve. A LinkedIn-style "self-reported" culture works for career networking; it's riskier for medical credibility, where an unverifiable claim of "published research" could mislead a patient into overtrusting a doctor.
Awards/memberships: same principle — clearly distinguish verified (Syndicate-linked or platform-confirmed) vs. self-reported fields in the UI data model, even if we don't build a full verification workflow for every field in V1.

New opportunity: Since Doctor Portfolio and Knowledge Center now share content infrastructure (articles/videos), a doctor's portfolio becomes a living, growing asset the more they publish — which directly solves the "ghost town profile" risk I flagged in Phase 1. Publishing an article isn't just Knowledge Center content, it's Portfolio content too — this dual-purpose design meaningfully increases the incentive to contribute.

4. AI Copilot (renames "AI Assistant" — Section 8 update)
Rename throughout: AI Assistant → AI Copilot.
Interaction model, formalized as a rule, not just a UI note: the AI Copilot must never be presented as a chat interface. It is a contextual side panel that surfaces relevant suggestions based on where the doctor is in their workflow (during consultation: live transcription + suggested questions + drug/allergy alerts; during note-writing: SOAP draft; during prescribing: interaction warnings and draft dosing). This is a meaningful product decision because a chatbot UI implies "ask it anything," which invites exactly the kind of open-ended diagnostic delegation we explicitly want to avoid (Section 8's governing principle). A contextual panel that only ever offers scoped, structured, approve-or-edit suggestions is a much safer product shape and a better UX — good call.
All prior AI feature list, business rules, and the "explicit doctor approval before record entry" governing principle carry over unchanged — this rename doesn't change any of the risk posture from Phase 1, only the interaction model and naming.
One addition worth naming: Since the Copilot is now explicitly a persistent panel inside the Doctor Workspace (Section 1 above), it needs a defined quiet/collapsed state — a doctor should be able to dismiss or minimize it without losing the "unified workspace, no context switching" property. An AI panel that can't be quieted becomes noise, and noise undermines the "calm, premium" philosophy from your Section 7.

5. Health Journey (new concept, restructures Section 2.7)
This is the most structurally significant change in this update — it affects the data model, not just the UI, so it deserves the most scrutiny.
Concept: Instead of one flat chronological Medical Timeline, each condition (hypertension, a pregnancy, a mental health course of treatment, a post-surgical recovery) becomes its own Health Journey — a structured, stateful arc: Diagnosis → Treatment → Follow-up → Monitoring → Resolution/Ongoing.
Updated module:
Health Journey
Purpose: Give both patient and doctor a condition-centric view of care, not just a chronological log — this is a meaningfully better mental model for chronic and multi-visit conditions than a flat timeline, and it's genuinely rare in existing telemedicine products.
Goals: Let a patient (and any subsequent doctor) understand "where am I in dealing with X" at a glance; let a doctor pick up an ongoing journey exactly where the last doctor left off.
Main features: Journey creation (triggered by a doctor's diagnosis during a consultation), stage progression (doctor updates stage post-consultation), multiple concurrent journeys per patient, a "Resolved/Ongoing/Chronic" status, journey-specific data (e.g., blood pressure readings living inside the Hypertension Journey specifically, not the general timeline).
Dependencies: Health Passport, Consultation Notes, AI Timeline Summarization.
Business rules (new — and this is where I want to push back a little):

Who decides a Journey's stage? Recommend: only the treating doctor updates journey stage, but a patient can independently log data into an active journey (e.g., home blood pressure readings) without changing its clinical stage. This avoids the failure mode where patient self-reported optimism ("I feel better!") accidentally marks a clinical journey as "Recovered" without a doctor's sign-off.
What happens when journeys should merge or split? Real medicine is messier than the clean example you gave — e.g., is "Type 2 Diabetes" one journey forever, or does each complication (neuropathy, a related cardiac issue) spin off its own journey linked to the parent? We need a real decision here before Phase 2 data modeling, not an assumption. I'd recommend a parent/child journey relationship (a primary chronic condition can have linked sub-journeys) rather than forcing everything into either "one giant journey" or "totally independent journeys" — but this is a genuine product decision with UX and data model consequences, worth a dedicated discussion before we lock the schema.
A single consultation can touch multiple journeys at once (e.g., a diabetes follow-up where the doctor also flags a new, unrelated skin condition) — the note-taking and journey-assignment UX needs to support "this note contributes to Journey A and creates new Journey B" as a normal case, not an edge case.

New opportunity: Health Journeys, done well, are genuinely more compelling marketing/demo material than almost anything else in this PRD — "see your hypertension journey, not just a pile of PDFs" is a strong, visual, patient-relatable pitch, and it's the kind of feature that could differentiate this platform in a pitch deck or portfolio context far more than another AI checkbox would.

6. Knowledge Center (new module)
Purpose: Turn doctor expertise into ongoing patient engagement and trust-building, independent of a booked consultation — directly addresses the "ghost-town profile" and long-term engagement risks flagged earlier.
Goals: Give doctors a reason to be active on the platform between consultations; give patients a reason to open the app even when they're not currently sick; build organic, low-cost trust signals ahead of a first booking.
Main features: Doctor-published articles, educational videos, health tips, FAQs; patient-side following of doctors, saving articles, sharing content (with appropriate link-sharing, not raw content export, to protect authorship); content feed personalized loosely by the patient's active Health Journeys (e.g., a hypertension patient sees more cardiology/nutrition content).
Dependencies: Doctor Portfolio (shared content infrastructure per Section 3), Patient Health Passport (for light personalization).
Business rules (new):

All published content must be attributed to a Syndicate-verified doctor; ghost-written or unattributed content is not permitted — the trust value of this module collapses immediately if content quality/authorship isn't controlled.
Content moderation queue in Admin (Section 2.15) needs a new content-review workflow — medical misinformation published under a doctor's verified badge is a serious platform risk, not a moderation nice-to-have. This should be treated with the same seriousness as doctor credential verification itself.
"Following" a doctor must not create an implicit ongoing clinical relationship or messaging channel outside formal consultations — keep the boundary between "content follower" and "patient" clear, both in the UI and the data model, to avoid the earlier-flagged risk of diagnosis-via-informal-channel creeping in through a new door.

Team challenge: Content moderation at scale is a real, ongoing operational cost most teams underestimate — recommend a lightweight pre-publication review for new doctors' first several posts (build trust in the moderation pipeline before opening it up), transitioning to post-publication spot-review once a doctor has an established track record. This is an operations design decision as much as a product one, and worth deciding now rather than reactively after the first bad post goes out under a doctor's verified name.

7. Product Philosophy (new cross-cutting principle, not a module — governs all of Phase 3/UX)
Noted and agreed — Linear, Notion, Stripe, Apple Health, and GitHub are a genuinely good reference set, and it's worth being specific about why each is relevant, so this isn't just a mood board:

Linear/Notion: speed, minimalism, keyboard-first efficiency — relevant to the Doctor Workspace especially, where a doctor doing this dozens of times a day needs low-friction repeatable flows, not a beautiful-but-slow interface.
Stripe: clarity and trust in anything touching money or sensitive data — directly relevant to Payments and the Health Passport's data-sharing consent UX, where clarity isn't a nice-to-have, it's a trust requirement.
Apple Health: the single closest reference for the Health Passport/Health Journey visual language — calm, personal, non-clinical-looking data visualization is exactly right for a "premium, calm, trustworthy" health record.
GitHub: relevant to the Doctor Portfolio specifically — a profile that shows real accumulated "contribution" (publications, articles, consultations) over time, similar to a contribution graph, could be a genuinely nice borrowed pattern for making a doctor's ongoing platform activity visible and rewarding.

This has one binding consequence for Phase 3: we should write an actual short design-language brief (tone, type, color, motion principles) before any screen design starts, not let five different modules independently reinvent "premium and calm." I'll flag this as the first task of Phase 3.

Additional Opportunities These Ideas Create

Portfolio ↔ Knowledge Center ↔ Journey personalization loop: a doctor's published article on hypertension can be surfaced directly inside a patient's active Hypertension Journey — this turns three separately-conceived features into one coherent trust/engagement flywheel, which is a stronger product story than any of the three alone.
Workspace as a doctor retention tool, not just a delivery tool: if the Workspace is genuinely fast and pleasant (Linear/Notion-grade), doctor day-to-day satisfaction — not just patient volume — becomes a retention lever. Most competitors treat the doctor side as an afterthought; this is a real opportunity to differentiate on doctor experience specifically.
"Contribution graph"-style doctor activity visualization (from the GitHub reference) as a lightweight, gamified but tasteful way to encourage ongoing Knowledge Center participation — worth prototyping in Phase 3.

Additional Risks These Ideas Create

Scope growth is real: Health Journey's parent/child data model, Knowledge Center's moderation pipeline, and the Workspace's "no context switching" UX are each individually nontrivial. Six months ago this was "book a video call with a doctor"; it is now a much more ambitious product. That's not necessarily wrong, but I want it named plainly before Phase 2 architecture, since each of these adds real schema and infrastructure complexity we'll be designing for next.
Mental health data handling now needs a genuinely separate consent/access model, not a checkbox in a general list — this is a compliance and ethical responsibility, not just a nice feature.
Content moderation liability (Knowledge Center) is a new, ongoing operational responsibility with real reputational and possibly regulatory exposure (medical misinformation under a verified badge) that didn't exist in the original PRD.

Final Recommendations for "Genuinely World-Class"

Resolve the Health Journey parent/child data model question now, in a short, dedicated product conversation, before Phase 2 — this is the one decision above that materially changes the database schema, and it's cheap to decide now and expensive to change later.
Write the design-language brief before any screens are designed in Phase 3, so the "premium, calm, trustworthy" philosophy is a shared, specific standard, not five modules independently interpreting a mood board.
Treat the Doctor Workspace's "no context switching" goal as a design principle to defend in every future screen review, not a one-time feature — it's the kind of promise that erodes silently over many small feature additions unless someone owns protecting it.
Keep the Knowledge Center's moderation pipeline scoped and real for V1 (even lightweight pre-publication review), rather than shipping open publishing and hoping abuse doesn't happen before you build moderation — in a verified-doctor-badge context, that's a genuine reputational risk, not just an MVP shortcut.

Ready to move to Phase 2: System Architecture whenever you'd like — and I'd suggest we resolve the Health Journey data-model question (item 1 above) as the very first conversation inside that phase, since it's upstream of almost everything else we'll design there.
